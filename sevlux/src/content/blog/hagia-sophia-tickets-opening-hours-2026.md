Hagia Sophia, one of the most significant architectural and cultural landmarks in the world, welcomes millions of visitors each year. Since its reclassification as a mosque in 2020, the visiting rules and hours have changed, and 2026 brings further updates. This guide provides the latest information on entrance fees, opening hours, prayer-time closures, dress codes, and photography rules. We share strategies for avoiding the long queues, the best times of day to visit, and what to look for inside — from the magnificent dome and Byzantine mosaics to the calligraphic medallions and the wishing column. Whether you are visiting independently or with a guided tour, this guide ensures you make the most of your Hagia Sophia experience.

## Hagia Sophia 2026 Opening Hours

In 2026, Hagia Sophia operates as a functioning mosque while remaining open to tourists during designated hours. The standard visiting hours are from 9:00 AM to 6:00 PM in the summer season (April through October) and from 8:30 AM to 5:00 PM in the winter season (November through March). However, these hours are subject to interruption during the five daily Islamic prayer times, which shift throughout the year based on the position of the sun.

The most significant closure for tourists is the Friday midday prayer (Jumu'ah), which typically takes place between 12:30 PM and 2:00 PM. During this time, the mosque is closed to non-worshipping visitors. Plan around this by scheduling your visit for early morning or mid-to-late afternoon on Fridays. On other days of the week, prayer-time closures are shorter — typically 30 to 45 minutes — and the site reopens once prayer concludes.

It is important to note that during the holy month of Ramadan, hours may be adjusted, and the evening prayer (Tarawih) can affect late-afternoon visiting times. Check the official Hagia Sophia website or ask at your hotel for the current prayer schedule before heading out, as times can shift by several minutes from week to week.

## Ticket Prices and Entrance Fees

As of 2026, entry to Hagia Sophia for prayer is free, as it is a functioning mosque. However, tourists visiting the monument are directed through a separate entrance that provides access to the upper gallery and museum-style areas. The visitor entrance fee is approximately 650 TL (roughly 20 USD at current exchange rates), which grants access to the interior and upper gallery where the Byzantine mosaics are located.

Tickets can be purchased at the entrance, but during peak season (June through September), queues at the ticket office can exceed an hour. We strongly recommend purchasing tickets online in advance through the official website or through authorized ticketing partners. Online tickets include a QR code that lets you bypass the ticket line entirely, though you will still need to pass through the security checkpoint.

Children under the age of 7 enter free of charge. Students with a valid international student ID may receive a discounted rate of approximately 350 TL. Turkish citizens with a valid ID card pay a reduced domestic rate. There is no separate ticket for the upper gallery — your entrance fee includes access to all visitor areas. Museums Pass Istanbul, which covers multiple historical sites, does include Hagia Sophia entrance, making it a good value if you plan to visit several attractions.

## Prayer Time Closures and Schedule

Understanding prayer times is essential for planning your visit to Hagia Sophia, as the site closes to tourists during each of the five daily prayers. The five prayer times are Fajr (dawn), Dhuhr (noon), Asr (afternoon), Maghrib (sunset), and Isha (night). The most relevant closures for daytime visitors are Dhuhr, Asr, and Maghrib.

The Dhuhr prayer is the most significant closure, especially on Fridays when the weekly congregational prayer (Jumu'ah) extends the closure to approximately 90 minutes. On weekdays, Dhuhr closures are typically 30 to 45 minutes. The Asr prayer causes a shorter closure of about 20 to 30 minutes, and the Maghrib prayer at sunset marks the end of the visiting day in winter.

To minimize disruption to your visit, arrive right after the morning opening or after the afternoon prayer (Asr) concludes. The exact prayer times change daily and can be found on Islamic prayer time websites or through apps like Muslim Pro. As a general guide, in summer the approximate prayer times are: Fajr 4:00 AM, Dhuhr 1:00 PM, Asr 5:00 PM, Maghrib 8:15 PM, Isha 10:00 PM. In winter: Fajr 6:30 AM, Dhuhr 12:30 PM, Asr 3:00 PM, Maghrib 5:30 PM, Isha 7:00 PM. Arrive 15 minutes before a closure ends to be among the first to re-enter, as the site fills up quickly after reopening.

## Dress Code and Visitor Rules

As Hagia Sophia is an active mosque, visitors must adhere to a modest dress code. Women are required to cover their hair with a scarf before entering the prayer area. Scarves are provided free of charge at the entrance, but you may bring your own if you prefer. Both men and women must cover their knees and shoulders — shorts, skirts above the knee, and sleeveless tops are not permitted. If you arrive wearing shorts, cloth wraps are available at the entrance to borrow.

Shoes must be removed before entering the main prayer hall. Plastic bags are provided to carry your shoes. Socks are recommended for comfort and hygiene, as the floor can be cold in winter. There are no locker facilities, so keep your belongings with you in a small bag. Large backpacks and suitcases are not allowed inside and should be left at your hotel.

Food and drinks are not permitted inside. Smoking is strictly prohibited. Talking should be kept to a low volume out of respect for worshippers, particularly during prayer times when visitors should remain in the designated tourist areas. The use of flash photography is not permitted in the prayer area, and visitors should not walk in front of people who are praying. These rules are enforced by attendants who are generally polite but firm.

## What to See Inside Hagia Sophia

The interior of Hagia Sophia is one of the most extraordinary spaces in the world, and knowing what to look for will dramatically enhance your visit. The first thing you will notice is the vast central dome, 31 meters in diameter and rising 55 meters above the floor. Designed to appear as though it is floating on light, the dome is pierced by 40 windows at its base that create a halo of natural light. Look for the structural buttresses added over centuries to support the dome, which has been partially rebuilt three times due to earthquakes.

The four calligraphic medallions, each 7.5 meters in diameter, are inscribed with the names of Allah, the Prophet Muhammad, his grandsons Hasan and Hussein, and the first four caliphs. These were installed in the 19th century by the calligrapher Kazasker Mustafa Izzet Efendi and are among the largest examples of Islamic calligraphy in the world.

In the upper gallery, the Byzantine mosaics are the highlight. The Deesis mosaic, dating to the 13th century, depicts Christ Pantocrator flanked by the Virgin Mary and John the Baptist. It is considered one of the finest surviving examples of Byzantine art. Other mosaics include the 10th-century panel showing Emperor Constantine IX and Empress Zoe presenting gifts to Christ, and the 12th-century mosaic of the Virgin and Child with Emperor John II Comnenus and Empress Irene.

On the ground floor, look for the Omphalion — the circular area of marble inlays on the floor where the Byzantine emperors were crowned. Also find the Wishing Column (also called the Perspiring Column or Column of St. Gregory) at the northwest corner of the building. Legend says that Emperor Justinian rested his aching head against it and was cured. Visitors today place their thumb in a hole in the column and rotate it 360 degrees to make a wish.

## Best Time to Visit to Avoid Crowds

Hagia Sophia is one of the most visited monuments in the world, attracting more than 3.7 million visitors annually. To avoid the worst crowds, timing is everything. The single best time to visit is right at opening — arrive 15 minutes before the posted opening time and wait at the entrance. Being among the first 50 visitors means you will have the interior nearly to yourself for about 20 minutes before tour groups begin arriving.

The second-best window is after the Asr (afternoon) prayer, typically around 3:30 PM in summer or 3:00 PM in winter. Many tour groups have moved on to other attractions by this time, and the afternoon light streaming through the dome windows is at its most beautiful. Avoid visiting between 10:00 AM and 12:00 PM, which is peak tour-group time, and avoid the period just before the Friday Jumu'ah prayer.

Weekdays are significantly less crowded than weekends, with Tuesday, Wednesday, and Thursday being the quietest days. Saturday and Sunday draw large numbers of local visitors in addition to international tourists. If you are visiting in summer (June to August), expect the highest crowds of the year; consider visiting in October or April when the weather is still pleasant but visitor numbers drop by roughly 40%.

## Photography Guidelines

Photography is allowed inside Hagia Sophia, which is welcome news given the extraordinary visual opportunities. However, there are important restrictions. Flash photography is not permitted anywhere inside the building, as the light can damage the ancient mosaics and frescoes. Tripods and monopods are also prohibited, as they obstruct walkways and create tripping hazards in the dark interior.

For the best photographs, use a camera or phone with good low-light performance. The interior is dimly lit, particularly in the upper gallery where the mosaics are located. Increase your ISO setting, use a wide aperture (f/2.8 or wider), and brace your camera against a railing or column for stability. The ground-floor views looking up at the dome are best captured with a wide-angle lens (24mm or wider).

The exterior of Hagia Sophia is best photographed from Sultanahmet Square at golden hour, approximately one hour before sunset. The warm light on the building's pink stone walls and minarets is stunning. For a unique angle, photograph the building from the rooftop terrace of one of the nearby cafes onAlemdar Street — many allow non-customers to access their terrace for a small fee or the purchase of a tea.

Drones are not permitted to fly over or near Hagia Sophia, as it is in a restricted airspace zone. Selfie sticks are technically allowed but frowned upon in crowded areas. Be respectful of worshippers — do not photograph people who are praying without their permission, and avoid taking photos during prayer times when the atmosphere is solemn.

## Tips for Visiting with a Guided Tour

A guided tour can significantly enhance your Hagia Sophia visit, providing historical context and pointing out details you might otherwise miss. Licensed guides in Istanbul undergo rigorous training and can explain the architectural innovations, the transition from cathedral to mosque, and the significance of the mosaics and calligraphy in ways that audio guides and guidebooks cannot match.

Guided tours typically last 45 to 60 minutes and can be booked in advance online or arranged on-site. On-site guides are available near the entrance and charge approximately 400 to 600 TL per person for a small-group tour. Private guides cost more but offer a fully personalized experience. Many guided tours of Hagia Sophia are combined with visits to the Blue Mosque, Topkapi Palace, and the Basilica Cistern, making for a comprehensive half or full day of Old City sightseeing.

The main advantage of a guided tour is skip-the-line access. Tour groups have a designated entrance that bypasses the general ticket queue, which can save you an hour or more during peak season. Guides also know the best route through the interior to avoid bottlenecks and can take you to the mosaics at the optimal time when the lighting is best.

If you prefer to explore independently but still want expert information, download a self-guided audio tour app before your visit. Several apps offer professionally narrated audio guides for Hagia Sophia that you can listen to on your own schedule. Alternatively, read up on the building's history before your visit so you can appreciate what you are seeing without needing to stop and read plaques in the sometimes-crowded interior.
